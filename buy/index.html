Common questions

What buyers usually ask.

What does "assumable loan" actually mean?
It means you legally take over the seller's existing mortgage — same balance, same interest rate, same terms. Instead of getting a brand-new loan at today's rates, you step into their 3.5% loan. You pay the seller their equity (the difference between the sale price and the loan balance) in cash, and from then on the loan is yours.
Do I have to qualify for the assumption?
Yes. The lender that holds the original loan has to approve you, and you'll need to meet their income and credit requirements — similar to qualifying for a normal mortgage. The difference is you're qualifying to take over an existing loan, not to originate a new one. This option is subject to lender approval.
Why is the cash needed higher for the assumption?
Because you're buying the seller's equity. The home is listed at $340,000 and the existing loan balance is about $295,000. The roughly $45,000 difference is the seller's equity, which you pay in cash at closing. In exchange, you get a payment that's about $585/month lower than a new loan would be.
What's the difference between assumption and seller financing?
With an assumption, you take over the seller's actual bank loan and deal with their lender going forward. With seller financing, the seller becomes your lender directly — no bank involved. Assumption gets you the lowest rate (3.5%) but needs lender approval and more cash down. Seller financing is more flexible on qualifying and down payment, but at a higher rate (6.8%) with a balloon payment due in 5 years.
How do I know which option is right for me?
It comes down to your cash on hand and your financial situation. If you have ~$45K for a down payment and can qualify with the lender, the 3.5% assumption saves you the most money long-term. If you have less cash or have trouble qualifying traditionally, seller financing gives you a path in with just $15K down. And if you prefer the simplicity of your own lender, a traditional purchase always works. Let's talk it through.
